Topic 29.2: PTQ vs Quantization-Aware Training (QAT)

Calibration Algorithms, the Non-Differentiable Rounding Barrier, the Straight-Through Estimator, and LSQ

1. 12-Year-Old Intuition: The Tightrope Routine

Imagine you are a professional gymnast who has practiced your entire life on a wooden beam that is 4 inches wide (FP32 precision). Suddenly, an Olympic judge gives you two options:

  • PTQ (Post-Training Quantization): The competition starts in 30 seconds. You must perform your routine, but the beam has just been replaced by a wire-thin tightrope that is only 1 inch wide (INT4). You are given zero practice time. What happens? Your foot slips by 2 millimeters and you crash off the balance beam.
  • QAT (Quantization-Aware Training): The judge gives you two weeks to train on the 1-inch tightrope before the competition. During practice, whenever your foot slips, your coach guides your balance back to center. By the time the real competition starts, you land your somersaults with absolute perfection!

3. PTQ Calibration Strategies

In Post-Training Quantization, static weights are known, but activation distributions depend on runtime inputs. Calibration runs a small unlabeled dataset to determine optimal clipping bounds $c$ and scale $s$:

1. MinMax Calibration

$$c = \max_{x \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}}} |x|, \quad s = \frac{c}{q_{\max}}$$

Guarantees zero clipping error, but is destroyed by extreme outliers.

2. Percentile Calibration

$$c = \text{Quantile}(|x|, 99.99\%)$$

Clips the most extreme $0.01\%$ of values, yielding $5\times$ to $10\times$ finer step size for the remaining $99.99\%$.

3. KL-Divergence Calibration (NVIDIA TensorRT)

Treats activation values as a probability distribution $P$ and finds threshold $T$ that minimizes information loss under quantized distribution $Q$:

$$D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) = \sum_{i=1}^T P(i) \ln\left( \frac{P(i)}{Q(i)} \right)$$

4. The Rounding Barrier & The Straight-Through Estimator (STE)

The derivative of the rounding function $f(u) = \lfloor u \rceil$ is zero almost everywhere ($\frac{d\lfloor u \rceil}{du} = 0 \text{ a.e.}$). Thus, naive backpropagation halts with zero gradients.

The Hard Saturation STE

The Straight-Through Estimator (Bengio et al. 2013) passes gradients through the rounding step as an identity operator while clamping outside the clipping interval:

$$\text{Forward}: \quad \hat{x}_{\text{fake}} = s \cdot \text{clip}\left( \left\lfloor \frac{x}{s} \right\rceil, \; q_{\min}, \; q_{\max} \right)$$ $$\text{Backward (STE)}: \quad \frac{\partial \hat{x}_{\text{fake}}}{\partial x} = \begin{cases} 1, & q_{\min} s \le x \le q_{\max} s \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

5. Learned Step Size Quantization (LSQ)

In LSQ (Esser et al. 2020), step size $s$ is trained directly via gradient descent:

$$\frac{\partial \hat{x}}{\partial s} = \begin{cases} q_{\min}, & \frac{x}{s} < q_{\min} \\ q_{\max}, & \frac{x}{s} > q_{\max} \\ \left\lfloor \frac{x}{s} \right\rceil - \frac{x}{s}, & q_{\min} \le \frac{x}{s} \le q_{\max} \end{cases}$$

With gradient scale factor: $g = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N \cdot q_{\max}}}$.
