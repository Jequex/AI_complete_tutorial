Topic 17.1: Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) Tokenizer

From Raw UTF-8 Text Streams to Subword Token Vocabularies with Zero Out-Of-Vocabulary Tokens

Executive Summary

A Large Language Model (LLM) such as GPT-4 does not process words, sentences, or English text directly. Computers operate exclusively on arrays of numerical values. Before a single neural network parameter or attention weight can be applied, raw text must be converted into a discrete sequence of integer IDs: $$\text{"Artificial intelligence is fascinating"} \;\longrightarrow\; [38241, 4434, 318, 14925]$$ This foundational translation layer is the Tokenizer. How text is partitioned into tokens dictates vocabulary size ($V$), sequence length ($T$), the computational cost of self-attention ($\mathcal{O}(T^2)$), and whether the model can handle rare words, multilingual text, code, or emojis without failing on "Out-Of-Vocabulary" (OOV) tokens.

The 12-Year-Old Analogy: The Secret Agent Word-Smushing Code

Imagine you and your best friend are secret agents passing handwritten notes across the classroom:

  • Method 1 (Letter by Letter / Character-level): You write out every single letter: T - H - E - - C - A - T - - S - A - T. It takes forever to write, your wrist hurts, and the teacher catches you because your note is three feet long!
  • Method 2 (Whole Words / Word-level): You make a codebook where every English word gets a number: the = 1, cat = 2, sat = 3. Messages become short, but the codebook is 500 pages thick! The moment someone writes slang, a brand name, or a typo like catzz, your codebook throws its hands up: [UNKNOWN].
  • Method 3 (The Word-Smushing Code / BPE): You start with just the basic alphabet (26 letters or 256 bytes). You notice you write t followed by h constantly. So you create a rule: Rule 1: whenever you see t and h together, smush them into a new symbol th! Next: smush th + e → the. Next: smush i + n → in, and in + g → ing!

Common words become single stamps, strange words like unbreakable split into familiar pieces (un + break + able), and alien emojis safely fall back to their byte blocks. Nothing is ever lost!

The Three Tokenization Paradigms

1. Character-Level           2. Word-Level                  3. Subword (BPE / Byte-BPE)
------------------           -------------                  --------------------------
['c', 'a', 't', 's']         ['cats']                       ['cat', 's']
- Vocab: ~256 chars          - Vocab: >500,000 words        - Vocab: 32,000 to 100,000
- Sequence length: Massive   - Sequence length: Short       - Sequence length: Optimal
- No OOV, but weak semantics - Massive OOV problem (<UNK>)  - Zero OOV, rich semantics

The Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) Algorithm

Given training text corpus $\mathcal{D}$ decomposed into raw UTF-8 bytes:

  1. Initialize vocabulary $\mathcal{V} \leftarrow \mathcal{V}_0$ with the 256 byte values ($0 \dots 255$).
  2. Iterate for $k = 1, 2, \dots, K$ merges: $$\text{freq}(u, v) = \sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \sum_{j=1}^{|c|-1} \mathbb{I}[c_j = u \land c_{j+1} = v]$$ $$(u^*, v^*) = \arg\max_{(u, v)} \text{freq}(u, v)$$
  3. Assign new token ID $t_{\text{new}} = |\mathcal{V}|$, record rule $(u^*, v^*) \mapsto t_{\text{new}}$, and substitute all adjacent $(u^*, v^*)$ pairs throughout the corpus.

Lossless Invertibility & Special Tokens

A production tokenizer must support special control tokens such as <|endoftext|> to delineate document boundaries without tokenizing them into character pieces. Furthermore, byte-level tokenization ensures that decoding is strictly invertible: $$\text{decode}(\text{encode}(s)) \equiv s, \quad \forall s \in \text{Unicode}$$
