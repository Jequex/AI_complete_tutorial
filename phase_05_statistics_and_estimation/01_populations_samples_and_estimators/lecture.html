Topic 5.1: Populations, Samples & Statistical Estimators

The inverse foundation of machine learning: inferring true data distributions from finite mini-batches, standard errors, and Bessel's correction.

1. Executive Summary & The Giant Soup Cauldron

In artificial intelligence, our goal is to build models that perform accurately across the entire universe of possible inputs (every photo, every question, every text token). This entire, infinite universe of possibilities is the Population.

However, computers can only ingest a finite dataset (10,000 images, 1 billion tokens). This finite subset is a Sample. Statistics is the mathematical bridge that allows us to infer true population parameters from finite samples.

The 12-Year-Old Analogy: The Giant Soup Cauldron

Imagine a chef cooking a 100-gallon cauldron of vegetable soup for a massive school festival (the Population). To know if the soup has enough salt, the chef does not drink all 100 gallons. Instead, they stir the pot thoroughly and taste a single soup spoon (the Sample).

  • If the pot is stirred well (random sampling), the taste of the spoonful gives an amazingly accurate estimate of the whole cauldron.
  • If you use a microscopic spoon (sample size $n = 2$), your taste test will be erratic.
  • If you use a large soup ladle ($n = 100$), your estimate will be almost perfectly reliable!

In AI, the Cauldron is the true data distribution $P_{\text{data}}$, the Spoon is the training batch, and the Taste is our estimated model weight $\hat{\theta}$!

2. Mathematical Definition: Population vs Sample

Property Population (The Reality) Sample (Our Observations)
Definition The entire infinite universe of possible data A finite subset observed in our dataset/batch
Size $N$ (often infinite) $n$ (finite batch size, e.g., $n = 64$)
Mean True mean: $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$ Sample mean: $\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$
Variance True variance: $\sigma^2 = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mu)^2]$ Sample variance: $S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2$
Status Fixed constants (ground truth) Random variables (change from batch to batch)

3. Mathematical Decoder Table

Symbol Mathematical Name Plain-English Meaning Type
$\theta$ True Parameter The hidden ground truth in the universe ($\mu, \sigma^2$) Fixed Constant
$\hat{\theta}$ Estimator A recipe/formula that takes sample data and outputs a guess Random Variable
$\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\theta})$ Estimator Bias Expected systematic error: $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta$ Scalar
$\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta})$ Estimator Variance Fluctuation of estimates across different batches Scalar $\ge 0$
$\text{SE}(\hat{\theta})$ Standard Error Standard deviation of the estimator: $\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta})}$ Scalar $\ge 0$
$\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta})$ Mean Squared Error Total error: $\operatorname{Bias}^2 + \operatorname{Var}$ Scalar $\ge 0$

4. Estimator Properties: Bias and The Error Decomposition

An estimator $\hat{\theta} = g(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ is called unbiased if its expected value across all hypothetical samples equals the true parameter $\theta$:

$$\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta = 0 \iff \mathbf{\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] = \theta}$$

The Bias-Variance Decomposition

The total expected square error of any statistical estimator decomposes neatly into two independent terms:

$$\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2] = \mathbf{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \Big( \operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) \Big)^2}$$

This is the mathematical origin of the famous Bias-Variance Tradeoff: in many machine learning estimators (like Ridge regression), introducing a small amount of bias allows us to dramatically slash variance, resulting in lower overall error!

5. The Sample Mean & The $\sqrt{n}$ Law of AI Data Scaling

The sample mean $\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ is unconditionally unbiased:

$$\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_i] = \frac{1}{n}(n\mu) = \mathbf{\mu}$$

Its variance shrinks inversely with sample size:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \implies \mathbf{\text{SE}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}}$$
The $\sqrt{n}$ Law of AI Data Scaling: To cut the uncertainty (standard error) of an evaluation metric or a gradient estimate in half, you must collect 4x ($2^2$) more data points! To reduce error by 10x, you need 100x more data!

6. The Grand Derivation: Why We Divide by $(n - 1)$ (Bessel's Correction)

Why does the sample variance formula divide by $n - 1$ instead of $n$? Let us prove algebraically why dividing by $n$ produces a biased estimate that systematically underestimates the true variance.

Step 1: Expanding around the Population Mean

Write each deviation relative to the true mean $\mu$:

$$X_i - \bar{X} = (X_i - \mu) - (\bar{X} - \mu)$$

Squaring both sides and summing over all $n$ observations:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu)^2 - 2(\bar{X} - \mu)\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i - \mu) + \sum_{i=1}^n (\bar{X} - \mu)^2$$

Notice that $\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i - \mu) = n(\bar{X} - \mu)$. Substituting gives:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu)^2 - 2n(\bar{X} - \mu)^2 + n(\bar{X} - \mu)^2$$ $$\mathbf{\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar{X} - \mu)^2}$$
The Key Insight: Because $n(\bar{X} - \mu)^2 \ge 0$, the sum of squared distances to the sample mean $\bar{X}$ is always smaller than the sum of squared distances to the true mean $\mu$. The sample mean sits right at the center of the observed points, artificially making them look closer together than they actually are!

Step 2: Taking the Expectation

$$\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2\right] = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[(X_i - \mu)^2] - n\mathbb{E}[(\bar{X} - \mu)^2]$$

Since $\mathbb{E}[(X_i - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2$ and $\mathbb{E}[(\bar{X} - \mu)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$:

$$\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2\right] = n\sigma^2 - n\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) = \mathbf{(n - 1)\sigma^2}$$

Step 3: Correcting the Bias

If we had divided by $n$:

$$\mathbb{E}[S_n^2] = \frac{n - 1}{n}\sigma^2 < \sigma^2 \quad (\text{Systematically Underestimates Variance!})$$

Dividing by $n - 1$ cancels the factor perfectly:

$$\mathbf{S^2 = \frac{1}{n - 1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 \implies \mathbb{E}[S^2] = \sigma^2} \quad \blacksquare$$

7. Practical AI Applications

  • Batch Normalization (BatchNorm): In PyTorch, BatchNorm uses $\frac{1}{B}$ during training for pure hardware speed, but when tracking the running population variance for inference (`running_var`), it scales by $\frac{B}{B-1}$ to maintain an unbiased estimate!
  • SGD Gradient Noise: The mini-batch gradient $\mathbf{g}_B = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^B \nabla \mathcal{L}_i$ is an unbiased estimator of the full dataset gradient ($\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}_B] = \nabla \mathcal{L}$).
