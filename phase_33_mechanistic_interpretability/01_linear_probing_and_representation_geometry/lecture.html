Topic 33.1

Linear Probing & Representation Geometry

Understanding the residual stream as a communication bus, the Linear Representation Hypothesis, probing mathematics, and selectivity controls.

1. Introduction & 12-Year-Old Intuition

Imagine you are looking at a bustling train station during morning rush hour. Thousands of passengers are moving through the central concourse, carrying briefcases, backpacks, cups of coffee, and tickets.

To an outside observer staring through the glass ceiling, the crowd looks like an incomprehensible, swirling cloud of chaos. But suppose you hold up a special pair of tinted red glasses: suddenly, you notice that every commuter heading toward Platform 4 is walking along an exact diagonal line across the floor. If you hold up blue glasses, everyone heading toward the exit is walking along a perpendicular line.

The Key Insight: The crowd was not disorganized—it was organized along geometric directions in space.

In a deep Transformer network, the sequence of hidden activations passing from input to output is called the residual stream. Think of the residual stream as a shared highway or communication bus. The Linear Representation Hypothesis states that deep networks represent human-understandable concepts as linear directions (1D vectors) in high-dimensional hidden spaces. A linear probe is a mathematical detector that tests whether a straight hyperplane can separate activations based on a semantic property.

3. Residual Stream as a Shared Communication Bus

In modern Transformers, layer outputs are added into the residual stream:

$$\mathbf{h}_L = \mathbf{h}_0 + \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \Delta \mathbf{h}_l^{\text{attn}} + \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \Delta \mathbf{h}_l^{\text{mlp}}$$

Every attention head and MLP reads from $\mathbf{h}_l$, computes an update vector, and adds it back into the stream. Because matrix multiplication reads linear projections $\mathbf{w}_j^\top \mathbf{h}$, features that align linearly with directions $\mathbf{v}_c$ can be retrieved instantly without multi-layer non-linear decoding.

4. Linear Probe Optimization & Gradients

Given activations $\{\mathbf{h}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ and binary concept labels $y^{(i)} \in \{0, 1\}$, a logistic probe models:

$$P(y=1 \mid \mathbf{h}) = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{h} + b) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{h} + b)}}$$

The objective function with $L_2$ weight regularization is:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, b) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ y^{(i)} \ln p^{(i)} + (1 - y^{(i)}) \ln(1 - p^{(i)}) \right] + \frac{\lambda}{2} \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$$

Analytical gradient for gradient descent updates:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (p^{(i)} - y^{(i)})\mathbf{h}^{(i)} + \lambda \mathbf{w}$$

5. Ruling Out Memorization: The Hewitt & Liang Selectivity Test

Why Accuracy Alone Is Insufficient

A high-capacity linear probe trained on high-dimensional vectors ($d = 4096$) can achieve high training accuracy simply by memorizing random associations.

To prove genuine linear encoding, we train a twin probe on randomized labels ($y_{\text{rand}} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.5)$). We compute:

$$\boxed{\text{Selectivity} = \text{Accuracy}_{\text{Real Task}} - \text{Accuracy}_{\text{Random Control}}}$$

A genuinely encoded concept exhibits High Selectivity ($\ge 0.35$), whereas memorization yields near-zero selectivity.
