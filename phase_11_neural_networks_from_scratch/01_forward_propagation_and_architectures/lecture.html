Topic 11.1: Forward Propagation & Neural Network Architectures

Mastering the artificial neuron, batch tensor transformations, and the mathematical foundations of deep learning architectures.

1. Executive Summary & The 12-Year-Old Analogy

A neural network is not a magical black box; at its exact mathematical core, it is a composition of parameterized affine transformations interleaved with non-linear activation functions. Before using deep learning frameworks, we must master the precise tensor transformations and dimensional algebra of the forward pass.

The 12-Year-Old Analogy: The Relay Race of Brain Cells

Imagine a massive school relay race where messages travel across stadium lanes:

  • The Sensory Observers (Input Layer): Three runners report current weather conditions: temperature (85°F), clouds (80%), and wind (20 mph).
  • The Committee of Listeners (Hidden Layer): In the next row, five students listen. Each has volume knobs for each runner: Listener 1 listens closely to clouds ($w = 0.9$) but ignores wind ($w = 0.05$). Each listener also has a personal stubbornness score called a bias ($b$): some speak with just a whisper, others require huge total excitement before uttering a sound.
  • The Baton Pass (Weighted Sum & Activation): Each runner multiplies inputs by weights, adds their bias ($z = \sum w_i x_i + b$), and tests if the sum passes an activation threshold. If excited, they shout their message forward.
  • The Final Call (Output Layer): The podium runners combine the hidden messages into a final forecast: "92% chance of rain!"
  • Batching (Parallel Lanes): Running 100 days of weather simultaneously across 100 side-by-side tracks is batch vectorization ($[B, d_{\text{in}}] \to [B, d_{\text{out}}]$).

2. Mathematical Symbol Decoder

$\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}$ Vector Single input sample with $d_{\text{in}}$ numerical features
$X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times d_{\text{in}}}$ Matrix Batch of $B$ training samples stacked as row vectors
$B \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}$ Integer Batch size (number of parallel observations processed)
$W^{[l]} \in \mathbb{R}^{n^{[l]} \times n^{[l-1]}}$ Matrix Weight tensor of layer $l$ connecting $n^{[l-1]}$ inputs to $n^{[l]}$ neurons
$\mathbf{b}^{[l]} \in \mathbb{R}^{n^{[l]}}$ Vector Bias vector of layer $l$ providing threshold offsets
$Z^{[l]} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times n^{[l]}}$ Matrix Batch pre-activation tensor: $Z^{[l]} = A^{[l-1]} (W^{[l]})^T + \mathbf{b}^{[l]}$
$A^{[l]} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times n^{[l]}}$ Matrix Batch post-activation tensor: $A^{[l]} = g^{[l]}(Z^{[l]})$, with $A^{[0]} \equiv X$
$\hat{Y} \equiv A^{[L]} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times n^{[L]}}$ Matrix Final predictions of the network for all $B$ samples

3. The Artificial Neuron: Biology vs. Mathematical Reality

While biological neurons communicate via complex biochemical action potentials, an artificial neuron is an affine map followed by a scalar squashing function:

$$z = \sum_{j=1}^{d_{\text{in}}} w_j x_j + b = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b$$ $$a = g(z) = g(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b)$$

The affine transformation $\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b$ rotates, scales, and translates the coordinate frame, while $g(\cdot)$ breaks linearity to permit non-linear decision boundaries.

4. Batch Vectorization & Tensor Bookkeeping

In modern deep learning, batching replaces slow scalar for-loops with hardware-accelerated BLAS matrix operations:

$$\boxed{Z^{[l]} = A^{[l-1]} (W^{[l]})^T + \mathbf{1}_B (\mathbf{b}^{[l]})^T}$$

Tracking the exact tensor dimensions at layer $l$:

$$[B \times d_{\text{in}}] \times [d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{out}}] + [1 \times d_{\text{out}}] \longrightarrow [B \times d_{\text{out}}]$$

The bias vector $\mathbf{b}^{[l]} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}}$ is broadcast across all $B$ batch rows, ensuring identical additive shifts for every example in the mini-batch.

5. The Universal Approximation Theorem: Why Depth Beats Width

Universal Approximation Theorem (Cybenko 1989, Hornik 1991):

A single hidden layer feedforward network with a non-linear activation function and finite width can approximate any continuous function on a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ to arbitrary accuracy $\epsilon > 0$.

Why do we use deep networks instead of a single giant wide layer?

  • Exponential Parameter Efficiency: Approximating oscillatory or compositional functions with a single layer requires $\mathcal{O}(2^d)$ neurons. A deep network with $L$ layers partitions space into $\mathcal{O}(k^L)$ regions with only $\mathcal{O}(k \cdot L)$ parameters.
  • Compositional Hierarchy: Natural data (images, speech, text) exhibits compositional structure: pixels $\to$ edges $\to$ parts $\to$ objects. Depth directly mirrors this hierarchical ontology.

6. Step-by-Step Hand Calculation

Given $\mathbf{x} = [1.0, 2.0]^T$, $W^{[1]} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -0.5 \\ 1.0 & 0.2 \\ -1.5 & 2.0 \end{bmatrix}$, and $\mathbf{b}^{[1]} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 \\ -0.2 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$z_1 = 0.5(1.0) - 0.5(2.0) + 0.1 = -0.4$$ $$z_2 = 1.0(1.0) + 0.2(2.0) - 0.2 = 1.2$$ $$z_3 = -1.5(1.0) + 2.0(2.0) + 0.5 = 3.0$$

With an output layer $W^{[2]} = [0.4, -0.8, 0.5]$ and $b^{[2]} = 0.3$:

$$z^{[2]} = 0.4(-0.4) - 0.8(1.2) + 0.5(3.0) + 0.3 = -0.16 - 0.96 + 1.50 + 0.30 = 0.68$$
