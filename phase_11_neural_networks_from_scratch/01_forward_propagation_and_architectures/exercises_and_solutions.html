Topic 11.1: Exercises & Solutions — Forward Propagation & Architectures

Rigorous practice problems covering batch tensor algebra, parameter counting, and the depth vs width expressivity theorem.

Easy

Problem 1: Manual Forward Pass for a Single Layer

Consider a batch of two input vectors $X \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3}$:

$$X = \begin{bmatrix} 2.0 & -1.0 & 0.5 \\ 0.0 & 3.0 & -2.0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Fed into a linear layer with weights $W \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3}$ and bias $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^2$:

$$W = \begin{bmatrix} 1.0 & 0.5 & -2.0 \\ -0.5 & 1.0 & 1.0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ -1.0 \end{bmatrix}$$
  1. State the shape of $W^T$, $X W^T$, and the final pre-activation matrix $Z$.
  2. Compute the exact numerical values of the matrix $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$.
View Complete Solution

Step 1: Tensor shapes:

  • $W^T$ has shape $[3, 2]$.
  • $X W^T$ has shape $[2, 3] \times [3, 2] = [2, 2]$.
  • Adding broadcast bias $\mathbf{b}^T$ results in $Z$ of shape $[2, 2]$.

Step 2: Linear product calculation:

$$X W^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2(1.0) - 1(0.5) + 0.5(-2.0) & 2(-0.5) - 1(1.0) + 0.5(1.0) \\ 0(1.0) + 3(0.5) - 2(-2.0) & 0(-0.5) + 3(1.0) - 2(1.0) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -1.5 \\ 5.5 & 1.0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Adding bias vector $\mathbf{b} = [0.5, -1.0]$ across rows:

$$Z = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 + 0.5 & -1.5 - 1.0 \\ 5.5 + 0.5 & 1.0 - 1.0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.0 & -2.5 \\ 6.0 & 0.0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Problem 2: Deep Network Parameter Counting & Memory Bookkeeping

Consider an audio network with architecture $[128] \to [256] \to [128] \to [64] \to [10]$ operating on batch size $B = 32$ with IEEE 754 32-bit floats (4 bytes each).

  1. Compute the total number of trainable parameters in the network.
  2. List the tensor shapes for $W^{[l]}$, $\mathbf{b}^{[l]}$, and $Z^{[l]}$ at each layer $l \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$.
  3. Calculate the total memory required to store the activation cache $\{A^{[0]}, \dots, A^{[4]}\}$.
View Complete Solution

1. Trainable Parameter Count:

  • Layer 1: $256 \times 128 + 256 = 33,024$
  • Layer 2: $128 \times 256 + 128 = 32,896$
  • Layer 3: $64 \times 128 + 64 = 8,256$
  • Layer 4: $10 \times 64 + 10 = 650$

$$\text{Total Parameters} = 33,024 + 32,896 + 8,256 + 650 = 74,826$$

2. Tensor Shapes:

  • Layer 1: $W^{[1]}: [256, 128]$, $\mathbf{b}^{[1]}: [256]$, $Z^{[1]}: [32, 256]$
  • Layer 2: $W^{[2]}: [128, 256]$, $\mathbf{b}^{[2]}: [128]$, $Z^{[2]}: [32, 128]$
  • Layer 3: $W^{[3]}: [64, 128]$, $\mathbf{b}^{[3]}: [64]$, $Z^{[3]}: [32, 64]$
  • Layer 4: $W^{[4]}: [10, 64]$, $\mathbf{b}^{[4]}: [10]$, $Z^{[4]}: [32, 10]$

3. Activation Memory:

Total floats: $32 \times (128 + 256 + 128 + 64 + 10) = 32 \times 586 = 18,752\text{ floats}$.

Memory: $18,752 \times 4\text{ bytes} = 75,008\text{ bytes} \approx 73.25\text{ KB}$.

Problem 3: Expressivity Analysis — Depth vs. Width

Let $g(x) = 2x - 4\max(0, x - 0.5)$ be a triangle sawtooth function on $[0, 1]$ having 2 linear segments.

  1. Prove that composing $g$ with itself $k$ times ($f_k = g \circ g \circ \dots \circ g$) produces $2^k$ linear pieces.
  2. How many layers and neurons are needed for a deep ReLU network to compute $f_k$?
  3. How many hidden neurons are required by a shallow 1-hidden-layer network to compute $f_k$?
View Complete Solution

1. Composing Sawtooth Pieces:

On each monotonic half-interval $[0, 0.5]$ and $[0.5, 1]$, $g(x)$ sweeps across the full range $[0, 1]$. Composing $g$ applies the two-piece fold to each half, doubling the linear pieces. By induction, $k$ compositions yield $2^k$ linear segments.

2. Deep Network Construction:

Each fold requires one 2-neuron ReLU layer. Thus, chaining $k$ layers uses $k$ layers and $2k$ total neurons ($\mathcal{O}(k)$ parameters).

3. Shallow Network Requirements:

A single hidden layer ReLU network $F(x) = c_0 + \sum_{i=1}^M c_i \max(0, w_i x + b_i)$ has at most $M$ breakpoints. To generate $2^k$ linear pieces, the function must have $2^k - 1$ breakpoints, requiring $M \ge 2^k - 1$ neurons ($\mathcal{O}(2^k)$ parameters). This proves that deep architectures are exponentially more efficient than shallow networks.
