Topic 8.3: Decision Trees & Ensemble Methods

From Gini Impurity and Shannon Entropy to Bagging, Random Forests, and Gradient Boosting.

The 12-Year-Old Analogy: 20 Questions & The Council of Friends

A Decision Tree is like playing the game "20 Questions". You ask: "Does it have feathers?" If yes, you ask: "Can it swim?" Each question cuts the possibilities down until you reach a pure guess.

Gini Impurity & Entropy: Think of a jar with 50 red marbles and 50 blue marbles. It's totally mixed up (high impurity!). If a question separates them into one pure-red jar and one pure-blue jar, impurity drops to zero. That improvement is Information Gain.

Random Forest: Instead of relying on one friend who might have bizarre biases, you ask a Council of 100 Friends. Each friend looks at different clues (feature subsampling) and reads different notes (bootstrapped data). When they vote, individual errors cancel out!

Gradient Boosting: Like an apprentice workshop: Apprentice 1 builds a basic model. Apprentice 2 inspects only the mistakes Apprentice 1 made and builds a tiny correction. Apprentice 3 fixes the leftover errors. Together, they achieve near perfection.

Mathematical Symbol Decoder

Symbol Mathematical Domain Description & Meaning
$p_k$ Probability $[0, 1]$ Proportion of samples in node $T$ belonging to class $k$
$G(T)$ Impurity $[0, 1 - 1/C]$ Gini Impurity: $1 - \sum p_k^2$
$H(T)$ Information Bits $\ge 0$ Shannon Entropy: $-\sum p_k \log_2(p_k)$
$IG(T, a, \theta)$ Impurity Reduction Information Gain from partitioning on feature $a$ at threshold $\theta$
$M$ $\mathbb{N}$ Number of trees in an ensemble
$\rho$ Correlation $[0, 1]$ Pairwise correlation between tree predictions
$r_{im}$ Residual / Gradient Negative gradient (pseudo-residual) for instance $i$ at iteration $m$

1. Mathematical Formulations of Impurity

For a node $T$ with class probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_C$:

Gini Impurity (CART): $$G(T) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^C p_k^2$$
Shannon Entropy (ID3 / C4.5): $$H(T) = -\sum_{k=1}^C p_k \log_2(p_k)$$

For a binary split dividing $T$ into left and right children $T_L$ and $T_R$:

$$IG(T, j, \theta) = I(T) - \left[ \frac{|T_L|}{|T|} I(T_L) + \frac{|T_R|}{|T|} I(T_R) \right]$$

2. Ensembles: The Variance Reduction Law

For an ensemble of $M$ trees with individual variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$, the total variance of the average prediction is:

$$\text{Var}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M f_m(x)\right) = \rho \sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{M} \sigma^2$$

As $M \to \infty$, the term $\frac{1-\rho}{M}\sigma^2 \to 0$. But the variance cannot drop below $\rho \sigma^2$! Random Forests resolve this by subsampling $k = \lfloor\sqrt{d}\rfloor$ features at each split, radically lowering the tree correlation $\rho \to 0$.

3. Gradient Boosting: Fitting Pseudo-Residuals

Rather than averaging independent trees in parallel, Gradient Boosting constructs an additive sequence $F_M(x) = \sum_{m=0}^M \nu \cdot h_m(x)$ where each stage fits the negative gradient (pseudo-residuals):

$$r_{im} = -\left[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}(y_i, F(x_i))}{\partial F(x_i)}\right]_{F = F_{m-1}}$$
