# Topic 03: NLP & Language Model Metrics

## 1. Introduction & 12-Year-Old Intuition

Imagine you ask a foreign language student to translate a Spanish sentence into English:
- Original Spanish: *"El gato negro duerme tranquilamente sobre la alfombra suave."*
- Professional Translation (Reference): *"The black cat sleeps peacefully on the soft carpet."*
- Student Translation (Candidate): *"The dark cat is resting quietly on the soft rug."*

Did the student get it right?
If you grade strictly with a multiple-choice rule ("did you use the exact word *carpet*?"), you would give the student a zero. But as humans, we understand that *"dark cat resting quietly on the soft rug"* preserves almost the entire meaning of the original sentence.

Evaluating language models presents a fundamental challenge:
1. **At the probability level**: How well does an LLM anticipate the next word? If a model has a **Perplexity** of 10, it means that at every step, it is as surprised as someone choosing a word at random from a hat with 10 words. Lower perplexity means the model understands grammar and facts much better.
2. **At the surface text level**: If the model generates a paragraph, how do we score it without hiring expensive human linguists?
   - **BLEU** asks: *"How many of the words and phrases generated by the AI actually appeared in the human reference?"* (Precision-focused, penalizing models that invent words or output short fragments).
   - **ROUGE** asks: *"How many of the important ideas from the human reference did the AI manage to capture?"* (Recall-focused, essential for summarization).
   - **Exact Match & Token F1** ask: *"In question answering, did the model pinpoint the exact span of text answering the query?"*

Understanding the mathematical mechanics, dynamic programming formulations, and pathological edge cases of these metrics is vital for evaluating modern LLMs.

## 3. Language Model Perplexity ($PPL$)

### 3.1 Derivation from Cross-Entropy Loss
Let a sequence of test tokens be $X = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N)$. The autoregressive language model assigns sequence probability:
$$P(X) = \prod_{i=1}^N P(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})$$

The average negative log-likelihood (cross-entropy loss in natural nats per token) is:
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = -\frac{1}{N} \log P(X) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log P(x_i \mid x_{<i})$$

**Perplexity** is defined as the exponentiated cross-entropy loss:
$$\boxed{PPL = \exp\left(\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}\right) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log P(x_i \mid x_{<i})\right) = \left( \prod_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{P(x_i \mid x_{<i})} \right)^{1/N}}$$

If base-2 logarithms are used (bits per token):
$$PPL = 2^{H(p, q)} = 2^{-\frac{1}{N}\sum \log_2 P(x_i \mid x_{<i})}$$

### 3.2 Information-Theoretic Interpretation
- **Branching Factor**: A perplexity of $K$ means that, on average, predicting each next token is as difficult as choosing uniformly at random among $K$ equally probable vocabulary words.
- A perfect model has $PPL = 1.0$.
- A uniform random guesser across vocabulary $V$ has $PPL = |V|$.

> [!WARNING]
> Perplexity is **only comparable across models sharing the exact same vocabulary and tokenizer**. A model with character-level tokenization will naturally have a lower per-token perplexity than a BPE word-piece model with $100{,}000$ tokens, because character vocabularies are tiny!

## 4. The BLEU Metric (Papineni et al., 2002)

BLEU evaluates machine translation and generation against reference human texts using **clipped $n$-gram precision** modulated by a **brevity penalty**.

### 4.1 Modified Clipped $n$-gram Precision ($p_n$)
Standard precision allows a degenerative model to output:
*Candidate*: `"the the the the the the the"`
*Reference*: `"the cat is on the mat"`
Unclipped precision for `"the"` would be $\frac{7}{7} = 100\%$!

To eliminate this exploit, Papineni et al. defined **clipped count**:
$$\text{Count}_{\text{clip}}(\text{n-gram}) = \min\left(\text{Count}_{\text{cand}}(\text{n-gram}), \; \max_{\text{ref} \in \text{Refs}} \text{Count}_{\text{ref}}(\text{n-gram})\right)$$

The modified $n$-gram precision is:
$$\boxed{p_n = \frac{\sum_{C \in \text{Cands}} \sum_{\text{gram} \in C} \text{Count}_{\text{clip}}(\text{gram})}{\sum_{C \in \text{Cands}} \sum_{\text{gram} \in C} \text{Count}_{\text{cand}}(\text{gram})}}$$

### 4.2 The Brevity Penalty ($BP$)
Without a penalty for brevity, a candidate outputting a single correct word (e.g., `"the"`) would achieve $p_1 = 1.0$.
Let $c$ be the total length of candidate translations and $r$ be the best matching reference length:
$$\boxed{BP = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } c > r \\ \exp\left(1 - \frac{r}{c}\right) & \text{if } c \le r \end{cases}}$$

### 4.3 Standard BLEU-$N$ Formula
BLEU combines $n$-gram precisions up to order $N$ (typically $N = 4$) via their geometric mean:
$$\boxed{\text{BLEU} = BP \cdot \exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^N w_n \log p_n\right)}$$
With uniform weights $w_n = \frac{1}{N}$:
$$\text{BLEU}-4 = BP \cdot \left(p_1 \cdot p_2 \cdot p_3 \cdot p_4\right)^{1/4}$$

If any $p_n = 0$, BLEU drops to $0$! (Standard implementations use smoothing methods like Lin & Och, 2004).

## 5. The ROUGE Family (Lin, 2004)

While BLEU is precision-driven (tailored for translation), **ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation)** is recall-driven, making it the de facto benchmark for summarization.

### 5.1 ROUGE-$N$
Measures $n$-gram overlap against references:
$$\boxed{\text{ROUGE-}N_{\text{recall}} = \frac{\sum_{\text{ref}} \sum_{\text{gram} \in \text{ref}} \text{Count}_{\text{match}}(\text{gram})}{\sum_{\text{ref}} \sum_{\text{gram} \in \text{ref}} \text{Count}_{\text{ref}}(\text{gram})}}$$
$$\text{ROUGE-}N_{\text{precision}} = \frac{\sum_{\text{cand}} \sum_{\text{gram} \in \text{cand}} \text{Count}_{\text{match}}(\text{gram})}{\sum_{\text{cand}} \sum_{\text{gram} \in \text{cand}} \text{Count}_{\text{cand}}(\text{gram})}$$
$$F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot \text{ROUGE-}N_{\text{prec}} \cdot \text{ROUGE-}N_{\text{rec}}}{\text{ROUGE-}N_{\text{prec}} + \text{ROUGE-}N_{\text{rec}}}$$

Common variations: **ROUGE-1** (unigram overlap) and **ROUGE-2** (bigram overlap).

### 5.2 ROUGE-L: Longest Common Subsequence
ROUGE-L does not require consecutive $n$-grams. It finds the longest common subsequence of words appearing in both sequences in chronological order.

Given candidate $C = (c_1, \dots, c_n)$ and reference $R = (r_1, \dots, r_m)$:
Using 2D Dynamic Programming:
$$L[i, j] = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } i=0 \text{ or } j=0 \\ L[i-1, j-1] + 1 & \text{if } c_i = r_j \\ \max(L[i-1, j], L[i, j-1]) & \text{if } c_i \ne r_j \end{cases}$$
The length of the LCS is $\text{LCS}(C, R) = L[n, m]$.

ROUGE-L metrics:
$$R_{\text{LCS}} = \frac{\text{LCS}(C, R)}{m}, \qquad P_{\text{LCS}} = \frac{\text{LCS}(C, R)}{n}$$
$$\boxed{F_{\text{LCS}} = \frac{(1 + \beta^2) R_{\text{LCS}} P_{\text{LCS}}}{R_{\text{LCS}} + \beta^2 P_{\text{LCS}}}}$$
Typically $\beta = 1$ for standard harmonic mean $F_1$, or $\beta \gg 1$ to prioritize recall in summarization.

## 6. Question Answering: Exact Match (EM) & Token-Level $F_1$

In reading comprehension (e.g. SQuAD benchmarks), model outputs are evaluated against gold answer spans.

### 6.1 Text Normalization Pipeline
Before string matching, both candidate $\hat{A}$ and gold reference $A^*$ undergo deterministic string normalization:
1. Lowercase text.
2. Remove punctuation characters (`!"#$%&'()*+,-./:;<=>?@[\]^_`{|}~`).
3. Remove definite and indefinite articles (`"a"`, `"an"`, `"the"`).
4. Collapse all contiguous whitespace to a single space.

### 6.2 Exact Match (EM)
$$\text{EM} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \text{normalize}(\hat{A}) = \text{normalize}(A^*) \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

### 6.3 Token-Level $F_1$
Let $T_{\text{pred}}$ and $T_{\text{gold}}$ be bags of tokens from the normalized strings.
Let $M = \sum_{t \in T_{\text{pred}}} \min(\text{count}(t, T_{\text{pred}}), \text{count}(t, T_{\text{gold}}))$ be the shared token count.

$$\text{Precision}_{\text{token}} = \frac{M}{|T_{\text{pred}}|}, \qquad \text{Recall}_{\text{token}} = \frac{M}{|T_{\text{gold}}|}$$
$$\boxed{F_1^{\text{token}} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{Precision}_{\text{token}} \cdot \text{Recall}_{\text{token}}}{\text{Precision}_{\text{token}} + \text{Recall}_{\text{token}}}}$$

If $M = 0$, $F_1^{\text{token}} = 0$. In multi-reference benchmarks, score against each reference and take the maximum:
$$F_1 = \max_{A^* \in \text{References}} F_1^{\text{token}}(\hat{A}, A^*)$$
