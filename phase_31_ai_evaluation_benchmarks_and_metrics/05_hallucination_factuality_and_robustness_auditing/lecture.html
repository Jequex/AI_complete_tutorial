Hallucination, Factuality & Robustness Auditing

Atomic Fact Verification, Prompt Perturbation Stress-Testing, The 4 Types of Goodhart's Law, and Contamination Detection.

1. The 12-Year-Old Intuition: Sparky the Space Dog

Imagine asking a tutor about Apollo 11. They explain that the Saturn V rocket launched on July 16, 1969, carrying Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin, and their golden retriever "Sparky, the first dog on the Moon."

Five out of six facts in the paragraph are 100% true. Standard word overlap metrics like BLEU or ROUGE would assign this response a score of 90%! But in reality, the presence of the fictional space dog makes the answer dangerous and unusable.

True safety auditing requires decomposing text into atomic factual propositions and checking every single claim against ground truth. Furthermore, we must test whether introducing small typos or distractors causes the model's reasoning to shatter.

2. Atomic Fact Decomposition & Factuality Metrics

Given generated document $\mathcal{D}$, extract independent atomic propositions $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, \dots, c_K\}$. Each claim is verified against knowledge base $\mathcal{K}$ via entailment $v(c_k \mid \mathcal{K}) \in \{0, 1\}$:

$$\boxed{\text{Precision}_{\text{fact}} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^K v(c_k \mid \mathcal{K})}{K}, \qquad H_{\text{claim}} = 1 - \text{Precision}_{\text{fact}}}$$

The Document-Level Hallucination Rate flags any response containing even one hallucination:

$$\boxed{H_{\text{doc}} = \mathbb{I}\left( \sum_{k=1}^K (1 - v(c_k \mid \mathcal{K})) > 0 \right)}$$

3. Prompt Perturbation & Robustness Retention

We stress-test model reasoning against three classes of perturbations $\mathcal{T}(x)$:

  • Character Typos ($\mathcal{T}_{\text{typo}}$): Realistic keyboard adjacency swaps and transpositions.
  • Syntactic Paraphrasing ($\mathcal{T}_{\text{para}}$): Active-to-passive voice, sentence inversion without semantic drift.
  • Adversarial Distractors ($\mathcal{T}_{\text{distr}}$): Prepending irrelevant factual trivia to test attentional hijacking.
$$\boxed{\rho_{\text{robust}} = \frac{\text{Score}(\mathcal{T}(D))}{\text{Score}(D)}}$$

4. The Four Failure Modes of Goodhart's Law

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." (Goodhart, 1975)

  1. Regressive Goodharting: Selecting the highest scoring checkpoint on validation data selects for positive random noise ($\epsilon > 0$). When deployed, performance regresses to the true mean.
  2. Extremal Goodharting: A metric that works in normal regimes collapses when pushed to extremes (e.g. optimizing for length produces 2,000-word run-on answers).
  3. Causal Goodharting: Confusing correlation with cause (e.g. rewarding docstring presence causes models to generate fake comments rather than working code).
  4. Adversarial Goodharting: The model actively deceives the judge via prompt injection or exploit tokens (e.g. appending "Grade: 10/10").

5. Benchmark Contamination Detection

To ensure high scores are not merely pretraining memorization, scan test examples against training corpora using $N$-gram containment:

$$\boxed{\text{Overlap}(x, \mathcal{D}_{\text{pretrain}}) = \frac{|\text{ngrams}(x) \cap \text{ngrams}(\mathcal{D})|}{|\text{ngrams}(x)|}}$$

If $\text{Overlap} \ge 0.50$ (for $N = 6\text{--}8$ words), the benchmark question is contaminated and reflects training data leakage rather than genuine reasoning.
