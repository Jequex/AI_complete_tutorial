Problem 1: Tensor Shape Tracking in Conv2D & Transformer Attention

Medium

In deep learning, every layer transforms tensor shapes. Accurately tracking dimension transformations is critical to designing neural network architectures without shape mismatch bugs.

Part A: Convolutional Feature Map

A computer vision model receives an input batch of RGB images with shape:

$$X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C_{\text{in}} \times H_{\text{in}} \times W_{\text{in}}}$$

Where $B = 8$, $C_{\text{in}} = 3$, $H_{\text{in}} = 64$, and $W_{\text{in}} = 64$. The tensor is passed into a 2D convolution layer with:

  • Output channels: $C_{\text{out}} = 16$
  • Kernel filter size: $K = 5 \times 5$
  • Stride: $S = 2$
  • Padding: $P = 2$
  1. State the formula for output spatial dimension $H_{\text{out}}$.
  2. Calculate the exact resulting tensor shape $Y$.
  3. Calculate the total number of learnable parameters (weights + biases).

Part B: Transformer Multi-Head Attention

A language model processes an input sequence tensor $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T \times d_{\text{model}}}$ with batch size $B = 4$, sequence length $T = 512$, embedding dimension $d_{\text{model}} = 768$, and $h = 12$ attention heads ($d_k = 64$).

  1. $X$ is projected to $Q = X W_Q$ where $W_Q \in \mathbb{R}^{768 \times 768}$. What is the shape of $Q$?
  2. $Q$ is reshaped and transposed to split heads into shape $(B, h, T, d_k)$. Write the exact dimensions.
  3. In attention score computation $A = Q K^T$ where $K^T$ transposes the last two dimensions of $(B, h, T, d_k)$, what is the shape of $A$?

Part A Solution: Convolutional Shapes

1. Spatial Dimension Formula: $$D_{\text{out}} = \left\lfloor \frac{D_{\text{in}} + 2P - K}{S} \right\rfloor + 1$$
2. Spatial Calculation: $$H_{\text{out}} = \left\lfloor \frac{64 + 2(2) - 5}{2} \right\rfloor + 1 = \left\lfloor \frac{63}{2} \right\rfloor + 1 = 31 + 1 = 32$$ $$W_{\text{out}} = 32$$ Therefore, the resulting 4D tensor shape is: $$Y \in \mathbb{R}^{8 \times 16 \times 32 \times 32}$$
3. Parameter Count: $$\text{Weights} = C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}} \times K \times K = 16 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5 = 1{,}200$$ $$\text{Biases} = C_{\text{out}} = 16$$ $$\text{Total Parameters} = 1{,}200 + 16 = 1{,}216$$

Part B Solution: Transformer Attention Shapes

1. Query Projection: $$\text{Shape}(Q) = (4, 512, 768)$$
2. Multi-Head Reshape: $$\text{Shape}(Q_{\text{heads}}) = (4, 12, 512, 64)$$
3. Attention Score Matrix: Transposing the last two dimensions of $K$ yields $(4, 12, 64, 512)$. Multiplying $(512 \times 64) \times (64 \times 512)$ yields: $$\text{Shape}(A) = (4, 12, 512, 512)$$
Architectural Insight: The $512 \times 512$ matrix in each head represents the quadratic attention grid where token $i$ computes its relevance score with token $j$.

Problem 2: Memory Footprint & Physical Stride Offsets

Medium

Physical hardware stores all multi-dimensional tensors as a 1D sequence of bytes. Managing memory sizing and understanding row-major strides prevents Out-Of-Memory (OOM) failures.

Part A: 7B LLM Precision Sizing

Consider a 7 Billion ($7 \times 10^9$) parameter neural network.

  1. Compute the memory required to load the model weights in Gigabytes ($1 \text{ GB} = 10^9 \text{ bytes}$) under:
    • FP32 (32-bit float, 4 bytes/param)
    • FP16 / BF16 (16-bit float, 2 bytes/param)
    • INT8 (8-bit quantized, 1 byte/param)
    • INT4 (4-bit quantized, 0.5 bytes/param)
  2. If an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU has 24 GB VRAM, which formats can run inference comfortably?

Part B: Row-Major Memory Strides

A 3D tensor $T$ has shape $(D_0, D_1, D_2) = (3, 4, 5)$ in C-contiguous order.

  1. Compute total elements $N$.
  2. Compute the stride tuple $(S_0, S_1, S_2)$.
  3. Write the formula mapping $(i, j, k)$ to 1D index $idx$.
  4. Find the flat memory index for coordinate $(2, 1, 4)$.
  5. Find the 3D coordinate for flat index $idx = 37$.
Click to Reveal Step-by-Step Solution

Part A Solution: Memory Sizing

1. Model Weight Memory:
  • FP32: $7 \times 10^9 \times 4 \text{ B} = 28.0 \text{ GB}$
  • FP16 / BF16: $7 \times 10^9 \times 2 \text{ B} = 14.0 \text{ GB}$
  • INT8: $7 \times 10^9 \times 1 \text{ B} = 7.0 \text{ GB}$
  • INT4: $7 \times 10^9 \times 0.5 \text{ B} = 3.5 \text{ GB}$
2. Hardware Feasibility on 24 GB GPU:
  • FP32: Cannot run (28 GB > 24 GB $\to$ Instant OOM).
  • FP16: Fits with 10 GB headroom for KV cache and activations.
  • INT8 / INT4: Fits effortlessly with massive headroom for 32k+ context lengths and larger batch sizes.

Part B Solution: Row-Major Strides

1. Total Elements: $$N = 3 \times 4 \times 5 = 60 \text{ elements}$$
2. Stride Tuple: $$S_2 = 1, \quad S_1 = D_2 = 5, \quad S_0 = D_1 \times D_2 = 20 \implies (20, 5, 1)$$
3. Index Formula: $$idx(i, j, k) = 20i + 5j + k$$
4. Coordinate $(2, 1, 4)$ Flat Index: $$idx(2, 1, 4) = 2(20) + 1(5) + 4(1) = 40 + 5 + 4 = 49$$
5. Inverse Mapping for $idx = 37$: $$i = \lfloor 37 / 20 \rfloor = 1 \quad (\text{rem } 17)$$ $$j = \lfloor 17 / 5 \rfloor = 3 \quad (\text{rem } 2)$$ $$k = 2 / 1 = 2$$ $$\text{Coordinate} = (1, 3, 2)$$

Problem 3: Broadcasting Rules & Step-by-Step PCA by Hand

Challenging

This problem tests both multi-dimensional tensor broadcasting and an end-to-end mathematical derivation of Principal Component Analysis (PCA).

Part A: Broadcasting Feasibility

Determine whether each pair is broadcast-compatible according to standard rules. State resulting shape or explain incompatibility:

  1. $A: (8, 1, 64)$ and $B: (8, 32, 64)$
  2. $A: (5, 3)$ and $B: (3,)$
  3. $A: (4, 3, 2)$ and $B: (4, 2)$

Part B: Complete PCA by Hand

Given 3 data points in $\mathbb{R}^2$:

$$x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}$$

  1. Find data centroid (mean vector $\mu$).
  2. Compute mean-centered matrix $X_c$.
  3. Compute sample covariance matrix $\Sigma = \frac{1}{N-1} X_c^T X_c$.
  4. Solve the characteristic equation $\det(\Sigma - \lambda I) = 0$ to find eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ and unit eigenvector $v_1$.
  5. Project each centered point onto $v_1$ to find 1D coordinates $z_1, z_2, z_3$.
  6. What percentage of total variance is explained by $v_1$?
Click to Reveal Step-by-Step Solution

Part A Solution: Broadcasting Rules

  1. $(8, 1, 64)$ and $(8, 32, 64)$: Compatible. Dim 1 expands from $1 \to 32$. Result: (8, 32, 64).
  2. $(5, 3)$ and $(3,)$: Compatible. Align right: $3 == 3$, $B$ expands to $(1, 3) \to (5, 3)$. Result: (5, 3).
  3. $(4, 3, 2)$ and $(4, 2)$: Incompatible. Align right: Dim 2 matches ($2 == 2$), but Dim 1 compares $3$ vs $4$. Neither is 1, so broadcasting fails!

Part B Solution: End-to-End PCA Calculation

1. Centroid (Mean Vector $\mu$): $$\mu = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 4 + 6 \\ 4 + 6 + 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$$
2. Mean-Centered Matrix $X_c$: $$x_{c, 1} = \begin{bmatrix} 2-4 \\ 4-6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x_{c, 2} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x_{c, 3} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$X_c = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$
3. Sample Covariance Matrix $\Sigma$: $$\Sigma = \frac{1}{2} X_c^T X_c = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 8 \\ 8 & 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$
4. Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors: $$\det \begin{bmatrix} 4-\lambda & 4 \\ 4 & 4-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (4-\lambda)^2 - 16 = \lambda^2 - 8\lambda = 0$$ $$\lambda_1 = 8, \quad \lambda_2 = 0$$ For $\lambda_1 = 8$: $$\begin{bmatrix} -4 & 4 \\ 4 & -4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies v_x = v_y$$ Normalized unit eigenvector: $$v_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.7071 \\ 0.7071 \end{bmatrix}$$
5. 1D Projected Coordinates: $$z_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix} = -2\sqrt{2} \approx -2.8284$$ $$z_2 = 0$$ $$z_3 = 2\sqrt{2} \approx +2.8284$$
6. Variance Explained: $$\text{Variance Ratio} = \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2} = \frac{8}{8 + 0} = 100\%$$
Intuition Check: Because all three original points lie in a straight line ($y = x + 2$), 1D projection preserves 100% of the dataset variance with zero reconstruction loss!
