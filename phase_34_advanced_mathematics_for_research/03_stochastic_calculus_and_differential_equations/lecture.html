Stochastic Calculus & Differential Equations

Core Research Question: Why does stochastic differentiation require the non-vanishing $(dX_t)^2 = \sigma^2 dt$ correction term in Ito's Lemma, and how do continuous score-based diffusion models reverse time to synthesize realistic data from Gaussian noise?

1. 12-Year-Old Intuition: The Drop of Dye in Water

Imagine dropping a tiny drop of dark food coloring into a glass of warm water.

Under a high-power microscope, the dye molecules undergo violent, chaotic collisions with trillions of jiggling water molecules every microsecond. This jittery random walk is Brownian motion.

Now take two steps back and watch with your naked eye: the individual random jerks vanish! The dye spreads outwards in a smooth, calm, predictable cloud. The microscopic chaotic jiggle ($dX_t = \mu dt + \sigma dW_t$) manifests macroscopically as a smooth partial differential equation of probability density (the Fokker-Planck equation).

In modern generative AI, Diffusion Models (Stable Diffusion, Imagen) use this exact mathematical duality: they dissolve images into pure Gaussian noise with a forward SDE, and then synthesize brand new images from noise by running the microscopic movie backwards in time!

3. Ito's Lemma: The Second-Order Differential

Because $(dW_t)^2 = dt$, the second derivative term in Taylor's series does not vanish when taking differentials of functions of stochastic processes:

$$df(t, X_t) = \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \mu \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} \right) dt + \sigma \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} dW_t$$
Geometric Brownian Motion Proof:
For $dX_t = \mu X_t dt + \sigma X_t dW_t$, let $f(x) = \ln(x)$.
$f'(x) = 1/x$, $f''(x) = -1/x^2$.
Substituting into Ito's lemma:
$$d(\ln X_t) = \left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) dt + \sigma dW_t$$ Integrating from $0$ to $t$ gives the exact closed-form solution:
$$X_t = X_0 \exp\left( \left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma W_t \right)$$

4. The Fokker-Planck Equation & Stationary Distributions

For an SDE $dX_t = \mu(X_t, t)dt + \sigma(X_t, t)dW_t$, the probability density $p(x, t)$ evolves according to:

$$\frac{\partial p(x, t)}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\mu(x, t) p(x, t)\right] + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\left[\sigma(x, t)^2 p(x, t)\right]$$

For the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process $dX_t = \theta(\mu - X_t)dt + \sigma dW_t$, the stationary density ($\partial p / \partial t = 0$) converges to:

$$p_{\infty}(x) = \mathcal{N}\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\right)$$

5. Reverse-Time SDEs in Diffusion Models

By Anderson's Theorem (1982) and Song et al. (2020), every forward diffusion SDE has an exact analytical reverse-time SDE:

$$d\mathbf{x} = \left[ \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}, t) - g(t)^2 \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_t(\mathbf{x}) \right] dt + g(t) d\bar{\mathbf{w}}$$

By training a neural network $\mathbf{s}_\theta(\mathbf{x}, t) \approx \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_t(\mathbf{x})$ using Denoising Score Matching, generative models invert the noise corruption process to produce high-resolution samples from pure Gaussian noise.
