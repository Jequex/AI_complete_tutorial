Spectral Theory & SVD Geometry

Core Research Question: How does singular value decomposition deconstruct arbitrary high-dimensional mappings into orthogonal rotations and axis stretches, and why is the truncated SVD mathematically optimal for rank-$k$ compression (Eckart-Young Theorem)?

1. 12-Year-Old Intuition: The Stretched Rubber Ball

Imagine holding a perfectly round, flexible rubber ball. This ball represents the unit sphere in geometry—every single point on its surface sits at an exact distance of 1 from the center.

Now imagine stretching, twisting, and placing this ball onto a table. What shape does it take? It transforms into a smooth ellipsoid (an egg-like oval in 3D).

No matter how severe or lopsided the transformation, three fundamental geometric facts remain strictly true:

  1. Orthogonal Domain Axes: Inside the original sphere, there was an orderly set of perpendicular axes ($\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3$).
  2. Principal Stretches: The linear transformation stretched each axis by a precise non-negative factor: the longest axis grew by $\sigma_1$, the next by $\sigma_2$, and the shortest by $\sigma_3$.
  3. Orthogonal Codomain Orientations: In the destination space, those stretched axes point in new perpendicular directions ($\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2, \mathbf{u}_3$).

This is the essence of the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD): $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U} \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^\top$. Every linear mapping is simply a rotation in the domain ($\mathbf{V}^\top$), independent scaling along coordinate axes ($\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$), and a rotation into the range ($\mathbf{U}$).

3. The Spectral Theorem for Symmetric Matrices

Let $\mathbf{S} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ be symmetric ($\mathbf{S}^\top = \mathbf{S}$).

Theorem:

  1. All eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ are strictly real.
  2. Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are mutually orthogonal.
  3. $\mathbf{S}$ admits orthogonal diagonalization: $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q} \boldsymbol{\Lambda} \mathbf{Q}^\top$ where $\mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}$.
Proof of Real Eigenvalues:
Let $\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$ for non-zero complex vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{C}^n$. Taking the conjugate transpose:
$$\mathbf{x}^\dagger \mathbf{S} \mathbf{x} = \lambda \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2$$ Taking the Hermitian adjoint of the left side (since $\mathbf{S}^\dagger = \mathbf{S}^\top = \mathbf{S}$):
$$(\mathbf{x}^\dagger \mathbf{S} \mathbf{x})^\dagger = \mathbf{x}^\dagger \mathbf{S} \mathbf{x}$$ Thus $\mathbf{x}^\dagger \mathbf{S} \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}$. Since $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 > 0$ is real positive, $\lambda = \frac{\mathbf{x}^\dagger \mathbf{S} \mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2} \in \mathbb{R}$. $\blacksquare$

4. The Eckart-Young-Mirsky Theorem

For any target rank $k < \text{rank}(\mathbf{A})$, the truncated SVD sum $\mathbf{A}_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^\top$ is the globally optimal low-rank approximator across both Frobenius and Spectral matrix norms:

$$\min_{\text{rank}(\mathbf{B}) \le k} \|\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B}\|_F = \|\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{A}_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i=k+1}^r \sigma_i^2}$$ $$\min_{\text{rank}(\mathbf{B}) \le k} \|\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B}\|_2 = \|\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{A}_k\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1}$$

5. Condition Numbers and Optimization Geometry

The condition number $\kappa(\mathbf{A}) = \sigma_{\max} / \sigma_{\min}$ measures how severely $\mathbf{A}$ stretches the unit sphere into an elongated needle or flattened pancake.

In second-order loss landscapes $L(\mathbf{w}) \approx \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{w}^*)^\top \mathbf{H} (\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{w}^*)$, the convergence rate of standard gradient descent is bounded by:

$$\|\mathbf{w}_t - \mathbf{w}^*\| \le \left(\frac{\kappa(\mathbf{H}) - 1}{\kappa(\mathbf{H}) + 1}\right)^t \|\mathbf{w}_0 - \mathbf{w}^*\|$$

When $\kappa(\mathbf{H}) \gg 1$, gradients oscillate violently across steep canyon walls while making negligible progress along the flat principal floor.
