Theoretical proofs and mathematical analysis for Temporal Difference Learning and Deep Q-Networks.

Exercise 1: Overestimation Bias in Q-Learning via Jensen's Inequality

Let $\hat{Q}(s, a) = Q^*(s, a) + \epsilon_a$ with independent zero-mean noise $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_a] = 0$. Prove that $\mathbb{E}[\max_{a} \hat{Q}(s, a)] \ge \max_{a} Q^*(s, a)$.

View Complete Solution

The maximum function $f(\mathbf{x}) = \max_i x_i$ is convex on $\mathbb{R}^k$. By Jensen's Inequality:

$$\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{X})] \ge f(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}])$$ $$\mathbb{E}\left[ \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \hat{Q}(s, a) \right] \ge \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \mathbb{E}[\hat{Q}(s, a)] = \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} Q^*(s, a) \quad \blacksquare$$

The maximization operator systematically picks the highest positive noise fluctuation, leading to overoptimistic Q-value estimates. This motivates Double DQN:

$$y^{\text{Double}} = R + \gamma Q\left(S', \arg\max_{a'} Q(S', a'; \theta); \, \theta^-\right)$$

Exercise 2: Semi-Gradient Nature of DQN Loss

Why does taking the full gradient $\nabla_\theta \frac{1}{2} (y(\theta) - Q(s, a; \theta))^2$ fail to minimize Bellman error, and why is the semi-gradient required?

View Complete Solution

Full differentiation with respect to $\theta$ yields:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -(y(\theta) - Q_\theta) \nabla_\theta Q_\theta + \gamma (y(\theta) - Q_\theta) \nabla_\theta \max_{a'} Q(s', a'; \theta)$$

The second term attempts to move the target toward the prediction rather than the prediction toward the target. In contrast, the semi-gradient treats the target $y$ as a constant supervisory signal:

$$\nabla_\theta^{\text{semi}} \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -(y - Q_\theta) \nabla_\theta Q_\theta$$

This guarantees the update implements a projected Bellman contraction step.

Exercise 3: Variance Comparison: Monte Carlo vs TD(0)

For independent transition rewards with variance $\sigma^2$, show that Monte Carlo return variance grows as $\frac{\sigma^2}{1 - \gamma^2}$ while TD(0) has single-step variance $\sigma^2$.

View Complete Solution

Monte Carlo return $G_0 = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \gamma^t R_{t+1}$:

$$\text{Var}(G_0) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} (\gamma^2)^t \text{Var}(R_{t+1}) = \sigma^2 \frac{1 - \gamma^{2T}}{1 - \gamma^2} \xrightarrow{T \to \infty} \frac{\sigma^2}{1 - \gamma^2}$$

For $\gamma = 0.99$, this variance is $\approx 50 \sigma^2$. The one-step TD(0) target $R_1 + \gamma V(S_1)$ has variance $\approx \sigma^2$, demonstrating why TD methods converge with much lower variance.
