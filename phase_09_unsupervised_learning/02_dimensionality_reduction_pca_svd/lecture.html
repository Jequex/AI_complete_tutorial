Topic 9.2: Dimensionality Reduction — PCA, SVD, & Subspace Learning

From Rayleigh quotients and Lagrange multipliers to the Eckart-Young-Mirsky Theorem.

1. Executive Summary & The 12-Year-Old Analogy

High-dimensional datasets contain rich signals tangled with massive redundant noise. Real-world data almost always resides along low-dimensional linear subspaces. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) discover the orthogonal directions of maximum variance and project hyper-dimensional observations into compact representations with minimal information loss.

The 12-Year-Old Analogy: Casting 3D Shadow Puppets onto a Flat Wall

Imagine holding a detailed 3D plastic toy airplane suspended in mid-air in a dark room. You want to record its shape on a flat 2D sheet of paper using a flashlight shadow:

  • The Wrong Angle: Pointing the flashlight directly at the nose creates a tiny circular shadow on the wall. You completely lose the wings and fuselage!
  • The Optimal Angle (Principal Component 1): Walking around until the flashlight captures the broad wingspan and long body yields a 2D shadow retaining 95% of the airplane's recognizable structure.
  • Reconstruction: Looking only at your 2D shadow drawing, anyone can reconstruct the original 3D toy airplane almost flawlessly, ignoring only the millimeter thickness of the plastic wing!

3. PCA from First Principles: Lagrange Multiplier Proof

Let $X_c \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ be centered. The variance of scalar projections $z_i = x_{c,i}^T w$ along unit direction $w$ is:

$$\text{Var}(z) = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_{c,i}^T w)^2 = w^T \left( \frac{1}{N-1} X_c^T X_c \right) w = w^T C w$$
The Rayleigh Quotient Maximization:

To maximize variance subject to $\|w\|_2 = 1$, we formulate the Lagrangian:

$$\mathcal{L}(w, \lambda) = w^T C w - \lambda(w^T w - 1)$$

Setting the gradient to zero:

$$\nabla_w \mathcal{L} = 2 C w - 2 \lambda w = 0 \implies C w = \lambda w$$

Pre-multiplying by $w^T$: $w^T C w = \lambda w^T w = \lambda$. Thus, the maximum variance is precisely the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ of $C$, and $w_1$ is its corresponding eigenvector!

4. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) Equivalence

Decomposing $X_c = U \Sigma V^T$, we compute the matrix outer product:

$$X_c^T X_c = (U \Sigma V^T)^T (U \Sigma V^T) = V \Sigma^T U^T U \Sigma V^T = V \Sigma^2 V^T$$

Dividing by $N-1$:

$$C = \frac{1}{N-1} X_c^T X_c = V \left(\frac{\Sigma^2}{N-1}\right) V^T \implies C v_j = \left(\frac{\sigma_j^2}{N-1}\right) v_j$$

Conclusion: Right singular vectors $V$ equal the covariance eigenvectors $W$, and eigenvalues equal $\lambda_j = \frac{\sigma_j^2}{N-1}$. Computing SVD directly on $X_c$ avoids forming $X_c^T X_c$ and prevents condition number squaring $\kappa(X_c^T X_c) = \kappa(X_c)^2$.

5. The Eckart-Young-Mirsky Theorem & Reconstruction Error

The rank-$k$ truncated SVD reconstruction $\hat{X}_k = \sum_{j=1}^k \sigma_j u_j v_j^T$ is the provably optimal low-rank matrix approximation in Frobenius norm:

$$\|X_c - \hat{X}_k\|_F^2 = \sum_{j=k+1}^d \sigma_j^2 = (N-1) \sum_{j=k+1}^d \lambda_j$$

The Explained Variance Ratio (EVR) informs how many components $k$ to keep:

$$\text{EVR}_k = \frac{\lambda_k}{\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j}$$
