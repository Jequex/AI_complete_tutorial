Topic 9.4: Density Estimation & Anomaly Detection

Mastering Kernel Density Estimation (KDE), Silverman's rule, Mahalanobis distances, and Isolation Forests.

1. Executive Summary & The 12-Year-Old Analogy

How do we model the continuous shape of data without making rigid distribution assumptions? And how do we catch rare, fraudulent, or dangerous anomalies? Density Estimation fits continuous probabilities over observations, while Anomaly Detection identifies deviations that violate expected correlations or densities.

The 12-Year-Old Analogy: The Airport Scanner Finding Anomalous Baggage

Imagine inspecting thousands of passenger suitcases passing through an airport X-ray scanner:

  • Kernel Density Estimation (KDE): Instead of guessing a rigid bell curve, you place a small glowing light (a kernel) on every suitcase. Where hundreds of bags weigh 40 lbs, their glowing lights blend into a bright sun. You get a continuous probability map without rigid formulas!
  • Mahalanobis Distance: Normal bags are either small & light (carry-ons: 20 in, 15 lbs) or large & heavy (checked: 32 in, 50 lbs). When a bag arrives that is 32 inches tall but weighs only 2 lbs, Euclidean distance looks normal, but Mahalanobis distance flags the broken correlation immediately: "A huge suitcase with zero weight is hollow and suspicious!"
  • Isolation Forest: You play a game using scissors to make random cuts across a dartboard. A lone outlier dot in the far corner gets isolated in 1 or 2 cuts (short tree depth), while a dot inside a dense crowd takes dozens of cuts. Fast isolation = anomaly!

3. Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)

The Parzen-Rosenblatt kernel density estimator constructs a continuous density from $N$ samples:

$$\hat{f}(x) = \frac{1}{N h^d} \sum_{i=1}^N K\left( \frac{x - x_i}{h} \right)$$

Using the standard Gaussian kernel $K(u) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^2}$, the optimal 1D bandwidth minimizing Mean Integrated Squared Error (MISE) is given by Silverman's Rule of Thumb:

$$h_{\text{opt}} = 1.06 \, \hat{\sigma} \, N^{-1/5}$$

4. Mahalanobis Distance for Correlated Outliers

Standard Euclidean distance ignores feature correlations. The Mahalanobis Distance measures distance in terms of the covariance structure:

$$D_M(x) = \sqrt{(x - \mu)^T \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)}$$
Chi-Squared Statistical Gating:

If inliers follow a multivariate Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$, then $D_M(x)^2 \sim \chi_d^2$. For $d = 2$ at the $\alpha = 0.01$ significance level (99% confidence):

$$\chi_{2, 0.99}^2 = -2 \ln(0.01) \approx 9.2103 \implies \text{Flag as anomaly if } D_M(x) > \sqrt{9.2103} \approx 3.035$$

5. Isolation Forest (iForest)

Anomalies are "few and different", which means they require far fewer random splits to isolate than normal instances. An observation's anomaly score across an ensemble of trees is:

$$s(x, n) = 2^{-\frac{\mathbb{E}[h(x)]}{c(n)}}, \quad \text{where } c(n) = 2\ln(n-1) + 2\gamma - \frac{2(n-1)}{n}$$
  • If $\mathbb{E}[h(x)] \to 0 \implies s(x, n) \to 1$: Definite Anomaly
  • If $\mathbb{E}[h(x)] \to c(n) \implies s(x, n) \to 0.5$: Normal Inlier
  • If $\mathbb{E}[h(x)] \to n - 1 \implies s(x, n) \to 0$: Dense Cluster Inlier
